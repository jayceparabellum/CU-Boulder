  • CLT for \(\bar X\) and for \(\sum X_i\), sums of independent normals
Convention trap: the sheet vs. your textbook notes
  • Geometric. The course sheet counts trials up to and including the first success: \(P(X=i)=p(1-p)^{i-1}\), \(i\ge1\), mean \(1/p\). Your Chapter 3 notes (from the Probability! textbook) call that "First Success" and use Geom for failures. The wording decides, not the name. The Module 4 homework asked for the "expected number of flips before we get a heads", which counts failures: \((1-p)/p\), not \(1/p\). "Until" or "including" means trials; "before" means failures.
  • Negative binomial. The sheet and the lecture both count failures before the r-th success: \(P(X=i)=\binom{i+r-1}{r-1}p^r(1-p)^i\), mean \(r(1-p)/p\). R's dnbinom(i, r, p) matches this.
  • R's dgeom(k, p) counts failures. For the sheet's geometric, use dgeom(i - 1, p).
  • The sheet's discrete uniform is on \(\{0,1,\dots,n\}\) (n + 1 values), but it prints \(P=1/n\). Read any discrete-uniform question carefully and compute from the definition.
Module 2

Descriptive Statistics and the Axioms of Probability

Know cold

  • Axioms. \(0\le P(A)\le1\), \(P(S)=1\), and for disjoint \(A_1,A_2,\dots\), \(P(\bigcup A_i)=\sum P(A_i)\).
  • Complement. \(P(A^c)=1-P(A)\). Use it for every "at least one" question.
  • Inclusion–exclusion. \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\), and
    \[ P(A\cup B\cup C)=P(A)+P(B)+P(C)-P(AB)-P(AC)-P(BC)+P(ABC) \]
  • Exactly one of A, B: \(P(A)+P(B)-2P(A\cap B)\). Exactly two of A, B, C: \(P(AB)+P(AC)+P(BC)-3P(ABC)\).
  • A single Venn region. "A and C but not B" = \(P(A\cap C)-P(A\cap B\cap C)\). Draw the three-circle Venn diagram and fill it from the centre outwards.
  • Sample spaces. Encode outcomes as 0/1 tuples (3 defects → 8 outcomes) and list events as sets. A sample space can be countably infinite: flip until the first tail gives \(P(n \text{ flips})=1/2^n\), and \(\sum 1/2^n=1\).
  • Equally likely outcomes. \(P(A)=|A|/|S|\).
  • Counting. Ordered without replacement: \(P_{k,n}=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}\). Unordered: \(\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\), with \(\binom nk=\binom n{n-k}\) and \(0!=1\). With replacement, ordered: \(n^k\).
  • "At least k" with combinations. Sum the "exactly" cases: \(P(\ge4)=P(=4)+P(=5)\). For "at least 5 of each" from a group of 11, list every split that qualifies (5–6 and 6–5).
  • Drawing one at a time vs. all at once. Without replacement, the probability is the same either way: \(\frac{20\cdot19\cdots15}{45\cdot44\cdots40}=\binom{20}6/\binom{45}6\).
  • "At least one group unrepresented" (homework): let \(B_i\) = no one from group i. Use inclusion–exclusion on \(P(B_1\cup B_2\cup B_3)\), where \(P(B_1)=\binom{n-n_1}{k}/\binom nk\) and \(P(B_1B_2)=\binom{n_3}{k}/\binom nk\).
  • Birthday problem (ungraded lab): \(P(\text{some shared birthday})=1-\frac{365\cdot364\cdots(365-N+1)}{365^N}\). It first passes 1/2 at N = 23 (0.507). With N = 10 it's 0.117.
Traps
  • "Does order matter?" decides permutation vs. combination. Committees and hands: no. PINs, rankings, seatings: yes.
  • Count the numerator and denominator the same way. If one counts ordered selections, both must.
  • "At most two of three" is \(1-P(ABC)\), not "exactly two."

Module 3

Conditional Probability

Know cold

\[ P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\qquad P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)P(B) \]
\[ \text{LOTP: } P(A)=\sum_i P(A\mid B_i)P(B_i) \qquad \text{Bayes: } P(B_j\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B_j)P(B_j)}{\sum_i P(A\mid B_i)P(B_i)} \]
  • Independent: \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\), equivalently \(P(A\mid B)=P(A)\). If A, B are independent, so are \(A, B^c\) and \(A^c, B^c\).
  • Mutually independent (three or more events) needs every subset to factor, not just the pairs. Lecture counterexample with two dice: E = sum is 7, F = first is 4, G = second is 3. Every pair factors (1/36 = 1/6 · 1/6), but \(P(EFG)=1/36\ne1/216\).
  • LOTP needs a partition: the \(B_i\) are mutually exclusive and their union is S. \(P(B_i)\) is the prior and \(P(B_i\mid A)\) the posterior.
  • Independence from a count table. Compare \(P(S\mid J)\) with \(P(S)\), or \(P(S\cap J)\) with \(P(S)P(J)\).
  • Chain rule. \(P(A_1\cap\dots\cap A_n)=P(A_1)P(A_2\mid A_1)P(A_3\mid A_1A_2)\cdots\)

Bayes problems in four lines

  1. Name the hidden cause (\(B_i\): which machine, disease or not) and the observed evidence (A: defective, positive test).
  2. Write the priors \(P(B_i)\) and the likelihoods \(P(A\mid B_i)\). A tree diagram makes this fast.
  3. LOTP for the denominator: \(P(A)=\sum P(A\mid B_i)P(B_i)\).
  4. Divide the branch you want by the total.

System reliability (from the lecture)

  1. List every path that makes the system work, e.g. {1,2}, {1,3}, {4,5}.
  2. P(system works) = P(at least one path works). Use inclusion–exclusion over the path events.
  3. Each intersection is "all components in those paths work". With independent components that's \(p^{\#\text{distinct components}}\). Count shared components once.
Traps
  • \(P(A\mid B)\ne P(B\mid A)\). A 95%-accurate test for a rare disease still gives a low \(P(\text{disease}\mid +)\).
  • Disjoint events with positive probability are dependent, not independent.
  • Prove independence by checking \(P(AB)=P(A)P(B)\) before you multiply.

Module 4

Discrete Random Variables

Recognize the story (formulas are on the sheet)

  • Bernoulli(p): one trial, 1 = success.
  • Binomial(n, p): number of successes in a fixed number n of independent trials.
  • Geometric(p): number of trials until the first success (sheet convention). \(P(X\gt k)=(1-p)^k\). Memoryless.
  • Negative binomial(r, p): number of failures before the r-th success. It's a sum of r failure-counting geometrics, which is why the mean and variance are r times those of one. NB(1, p) = (sheet geometric) − 1.
  • Discrete uniform on {0, …, 9} (lecture example): P = 1/10 each, E = 4.5, E[X²] = 28.5, Var = 8.25. The sheet's mean n/2 and variance n(n+2)/12 are right with n = 9, but each probability is 1/(n+1), not the 1/n it prints.
  • Poisson(λ): count of rare events in a fixed interval at average rate λ. Approximates Bin(n, p) with λ = np when n is large and p small.

Know cold

  • Valid pmf: \(p(x)\ge0\) and \(\sum p(x)=1\). Use this to find an unknown constant.
  • CDF: \(F(x)=P(X\le x)\), a step function for discrete X. \(P(a\lt X\le b)=F(b)-F(a)\).
  • \(E[X]=\sum x\,p(x)\), \(E[g(X)]=\sum g(x)p(x)\), \(\mathrm{Var}[X]=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2\).
  • \(E[aX+b]=aE[X]+b\), \(\mathrm{Var}[aX+b]=a^2\mathrm{Var}[X]\), \(\mathrm{SD}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}}\).
Traps
  • For discrete X, \(P(X\lt 3)\ne P(X\le3)\). Write out which integers are included. "At least 3" = \(1-P(X\le2)\).
  • Binomial needs a fixed n. "Until the 5th head" is negative binomial; "until the first head" is geometric.
  • \(\mathrm{Var}[-X]=\mathrm{Var}[X]\), and \(\mathrm{Var}[X-Y]\) adds variances (when independent).
  • "15 people must be asked before 5 are found" is ambiguous. If 15 people are asked in total, that's 10 failures. The lecture plugged 15 in as the number of failures. Read the wording, decide what is being counted, and write it down.

Module 5

Continuous Random Variables

Know cold

  • Valid pdf: \(f\ge0\), \(\int f=1\). \(P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)\), and \(P(X=a)=0\), so \(\lt\) and \(\le\) are interchangeable.
  • \(F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f(t)\,dt\), \(f(x)=F'(x)\). \(E[X]=\int xf(x)\,dx\), \(E[g(X)]=\int g(x)f(x)\,dx\).
  • Percentile: the p-th quantile solves \(F(x_p)=p\). The median solves \(F(m)=0.5\).
  • Exponential(λ): \(F(x)=1-e^{-\lambda x}\), \(P(X\gt x)=e^{-\lambda x}\). Memoryless: \(P(X\gt s+t\mid X\gt s)=P(X\gt t)\). If the problem gives a mean of 10, then λ = 1/10.
  • Uniform(a, b): \(F(x)=\frac{x-a}{b-a}\) on (a, b). Probability = length / (b − a).
  • Exponential by integration (the lecture derives these): \(\int_0^\infty\lambda e^{-\lambda x}dx=1\), \(E[X]=1/\lambda\), \(E[X^2]=2/\lambda^2\) by parts, and memorylessness from \(e^{-\lambda(s+t)}/e^{-\lambda s}=e^{-\lambda t}\).
  • Poisson ↔ exponential. The same λ per unit time: 5 customers per hour means an average gap of 1/5 hour between customers.
  • Rescale λ to the time window. In the homework, accidents average 5 per week, so the wait for the next one is Exp(5) in weeks. P(wait ≥ 1 week) = \(e^{-5}\), and P(no accident for 2 more weeks) = \(e^{-5\cdot2}=e^{-10}\) by memorylessness, however long it's already been.
  • \(E[g(X)]\ne g(E[X])\). A die pays \(1/X\) dollars: \(E[1/X]=\frac16\sum_{k=1}^6\frac1k\approx0.408\), not \(1/3.5\approx0.286\). That's better than a guaranteed $0.25.
  • Linear transformation of a normal (ungraded lab): if Z ~ N(0, 1), then 2Z − 1 ~ N(−1, 4), with mean −1 and variance \(2^2\).
  • Modelling assumptions. A Poisson count has infinite support, and a normal model of a positive quantity (like reaction time) allows negative values. Both are fine when those tails have negligible probability.

Normal distribution

\[ Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1),\qquad P(X\le x)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right),\qquad \Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z),\qquad x_p=\mu+z_p\sigma \]
  • Landmarks: \(\Phi(1.645)=0.95\), \(\Phi(1.96)=0.975\), \(\Phi(2.33)\approx0.99\), \(\Phi(2.576)=0.995\). Lecture values: within ±1σ is 0.6826, within ±2σ is 0.954, and \(P(-1.645\lt Z\lt1.645)=0.90\).
  • Shape. Symmetric about μ, inflection points at μ ± σ, and a smaller σ gives a taller, narrower curve.
  • \(aX+b\sim N(a\mu+b,\ a^2\sigma^2)\).
  • Reading the Φ table. The row gives the units and tenths, the column the hundredths: \(\Phi(0.23)\) is row 0.2, column 0.03 = 0.5910. For an inverse lookup that falls between two entries, interpolate. The lecture gets \(\Phi(z)=0.7\Rightarrow z\approx0.525\), halfway between 0.52 and 0.53. In the exam, qnorm(0.7) = 0.5244 is faster.
  • The lecture's zα means \(\Phi(z_\alpha)=\alpha\), a lower-tail area: "α = 0.95 gives z = 1.645". That's the opposite of the upper-tail convention used in APPA 5003.

Normal approximation to the binomial

If \(X\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)\) and \(np(1-p)\ge10\) (the lecture's rule), then \(X\approx N(np,\ np(1-p))\). Because X is discrete, widen each end by 0.5 (continuity correction):

\[ P(X\ge k)\approx1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{k-0.5-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right),\qquad P(X\le k)\approx\Phi\!\left(\frac{k+0.5-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right) \]
\[ P(a\le X\le b)\approx\Phi\!\left(\frac{b+0.5-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right)-\Phi\!\left(\frac{a-0.5-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right) \]
Traps
  • \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\): the second number is the variance. R's pnorm(x, mean, sd) takes the standard deviation, so pnorm(62, 50, 10) for N(50, 100).
  • R's pexp(x, rate) takes the rate λ, not the mean.
  • A pdf can be bigger than 1. A probability can't.
  • Piecewise pdfs: integrate only over the region where f is nonzero, and split the integral at the break points.

Module 6

Joint Distributions and Covariance

Know cold

  • Joint pmf \(p(x,y)=P(X=x,Y=y)\), summing to 1 over the table. Marginals: row and column sums, \(p_X(x)=\sum_y p(x,y)\).
  • Independent iff \(p(x,y)=p_X(x)p_Y(y)\) for every cell. One failing cell proves dependence.
  • \(E[g(X,Y)]=\sum_x\sum_y g(x,y)\,p(x,y)\). In particular \(E[XY]=\sum\sum xy\,p(x,y)\).
  • Covariance. \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]\). \(\mathrm{Cov}(aX+b,cY+d)=ac\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)\), \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,X)=\mathrm{Var}[X]\).
  • Correlation. \(\rho=\dfrac{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}\in[-1,1]\), unitless, ±1 only for an exact linear relationship.
  • Variance of a combination. \(\mathrm{Var}[aX+bY]=a^2\mathrm{Var}[X]+b^2\mathrm{Var}[Y]+2ab\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)\).
  • Covariance by definition. \(\sum\sum(x-\mu_X)(y-\mu_Y)p(x,y)\). Positive when points sit mostly in the "both above" or "both below" quadrants. A strong curved relationship can still have Cov ≈ 0.
  • ρ = ±1 for Y = aX + b. \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,aX+b)=a\sigma_X^2\) and \(\sigma_Y=|a|\sigma_X\), so ρ = sign(a).
  • Var of g(X). Find \(E[g(X)]\) first, then \(\sum(g(x)-E[g(X)])^2p(x)\), or use \(\mathrm{Var}[aX+b]=a^2\mathrm{Var}[X]\) when g is linear.

Continuous joint distributions

\(P(a\lt X\lt b,\ c\lt Y\lt d)=\int_a^b\!\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx\). X and Y are independent iff \(f(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)\). In that case the double integral splits into a product: \(P(X\in A,\ Y\in B)=P(X\in A)\,P(Y\in B)\). The lecture only uses this factored form.

Traps
  • Independent ⇒ Cov = 0, but Cov = 0 doesn't imply independent.
  • \(\mathrm{Var}[X-Y]=\mathrm{Var}[X]+\mathrm{Var}[Y]-2\mathrm{Cov}\). The variances still add.
  • For ρ, use standard deviations (square roots), not variances.
  • Changing units changes the covariance but not ρ: \(\mathrm{Cov}(100X,100Y)=10^4\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)\), while ρ stays the same (homework).
  • Given ρ and two SDs, first get \(\mathrm{Cov}=\rho\,\sigma_X\sigma_Y\), then use the Var formula. The SD of a combination is the square root at the very end.

Module 7

The Central Limit Theorem

Know cold

  • Random sample: \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) iid. Then \(E[\bar X]=\mu\) and \(\mathrm{Var}[\bar X]=\sigma^2/n\), because independence removes every covariance term.
  • Law of Large Numbers. \(\bar X\) itself converges to μ as n grows (iid Unif(0, 1) gives \(\bar X\to1/2\)). LLN says where \(\bar X\) goes; the CLT describes the shape of its spread.
  • Normal data: if the \(X_i\) are iid N(μ, σ²), then \(\bar X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)\) exactly, for any n.
  • Sums of independent normals are exactly normal. \(\sum c_iX_i\sim N\big(\sum c_i\mu_i,\ \sum c_i^2\sigma_i^2\big)\).
  • CLT. For iid \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) with mean μ, variance σ², and n large (rule of thumb n ≥ 30):
\[ \bar X\approx N\!\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right),\qquad \sum_{i=1}^n X_i\approx N(n\mu,\ n\sigma^2) \]
  • The more skewed the original distribution, the larger n needs to be.
  • The normal approximation to the binomial (with continuity correction) is the CLT applied to a sum of Bernoullis.

CLT problems in four lines

  1. Find μ and σ² for one observation.
  2. Decide: mean or sum? Scale to σ²/n (mean) or nσ² (sum).
  3. Standardize with the standard deviation of that mean or sum.
  4. Read Φ, or use pnorm. Add a continuity correction if the sum is integer-valued.
Traps
  • \(\mathrm{SD}(\bar X)=\sigma/\sqrt n\), not σ/n.
  • "Variance is 0.5" means σ² = 0.5, so \(\mathrm{Var}[\bar X]=0.5/n\).
  • The CLT is about the mean or sum. A single observation from a skewed distribution doesn't become normal.
  • The lecture rounds \(z_{0.99}\) to 2.33 (table value); R gives 2.326. On the errands example that's 52.76 vs. 52.74 min. Carry R's value unless a question tells you to use the table, and don't round z until the end.
  • \(X_1+X_2\ne2X_1\). For independent copies, \(\mathrm{Var}(X_1+X_2)=2\sigma^2\), but \(\mathrm{Var}(2X_1)=4\sigma^2\). The homework's fishing problem warned that 122 is wrong for exactly this reason: three catches is a sum of three independent times, not 3 × one time.
  • Difference of two sample means from independent groups: \(\bar Y-\bar X\approx N\big(\mu_Y-\mu_X,\ \sigma_Y^2/n_Y+\sigma_X^2/n_X\big)\). The variances add.
  • The lecture writes "binomial 19.08 and 8.97" for a binomial with mean 19.08 and variance 8.97. Bin(n, p) takes n and p, so don't copy that notation.

P <- matrix(c(.10, .20, .10,
              .20, .30, .10), nrow = 2, byrow = TRUE)   # rows X = 0,1; cols Y = 0,1,2
x <- 0:1; y <- 0:2
px <- rowSums(P); py <- colSums(P)
